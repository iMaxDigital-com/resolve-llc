<h1>Should I Stay or Should I Go?</h1>
<p><em>When an exciting career opportunity required looking beyond the headline offer.</em></p>

<p>Seema was in her early 30s and a successful software engineer at one of the world’s leading technology companies. She was well compensated, highly regarded, and on a strong career trajectory. She also had meaningful unvested equity that she would leave behind if she departed.</p>

<p>Then an interesting opportunity came along.</p>

<p>A growing startup wanted Seema to join its team. She knew and respected some of the people involved, liked what the company was building, and could see the potential upside if the business succeeded. The opportunity offered something her current position could not: the chance to join a smaller company at an earlier stage and help build something significant.</p>

<p>Seema was struggling with the decision, and her roommate referred her to me.</p>

<p>The question seemed straightforward: <strong>Should she stay or should she go?</strong></p>

<p>But the alternatives weren't directly comparable. Staying meant retaining compensation, equity, stability and a career path that already had substantial value. Going meant accepting greater uncertainty in exchange for professional opportunity and potentially significant financial upside.</p>

<p>The challenge wasn't simply determining which job paid more. It was understanding what Seema would be giving up, what she would be getting in return, and how much additional risk she was being compensated to take.</p>

<p>And the decision couldn't be made with a spreadsheet alone. It required some soul-searching and answering more fundamental questions: <strong>What do I want? What makes me happy? What am I looking for at this point in my career? What am I willing to give up to get it?</strong></p>

<p>Pondering those questions put Seema's emotions into overdrive. Excitement about the opportunity competed with anxiety about leaving a good situation, fear of making the wrong decision, and the possibility that whichever path she chose, she might regret the one she didn't take.</p>

<p>Part of our work was helping Seema understand those emotions without allowing them to make the decision for her. Before she could decide which opportunity was better, she needed to think more clearly about <strong>what “better” meant to her.</strong></p>

<p>We then turned to the economics. We established the value of what Seema already had and what she could reasonably expect if she stayed. We looked at the startup across a range of possible outcomes rather than treating the potential value of its equity as though it were certain.</p>

<p>In effect, we reframed the economic analysis as an investment decision: <strong>What was she being asked to invest? What was she giving up to make that investment? What were the potential returns? Were those returns sufficient to compensate her for the risk?</strong></p>

<p>The analysis also exposed things she didn't yet know about the startup. We developed a due diligence list covering its capitalization, potential dilution, hiring plans, financial projections and expectations for future financing.</p>

<p>There is an important difference between <strong>uncertainty and missing information</strong>. Uncertainty is unavoidable when considering an early-stage company. Missing information can often be addressed by asking better questions.</p>

<p>As Seema worked through the questions, the decision became clearer.</p>

<p>She liked the company and respected the people. She could imagine enjoying the role and believed there was a credible path to a successful outcome. But believing an opportunity could work wasn't the same as concluding that she should take it.</p>

<p>Given what she would be leaving behind, the risks she would be assuming, and what she had come to understand about what mattered to her, the offer wasn't compelling enough.</p>

<p>Seema decided to stay.</p>

<p>She explained that the package, as structured, didn't justify making the move. She left the door open to reconsider if the economics changed materially and preserved a relationship she valued.</p>

<p>There was no way to know whether staying would ultimately prove to be the financially superior choice. That wasn't the standard.</p>

<p>Seema had reached a decision she understood. She knew what she was giving up, what she might gain, what she believed, what she didn't know and, perhaps most importantly, <strong>what she wanted.</strong></p>

<p><strong>Good judgment doesn't require knowing what will happen. It requires understanding what matters to you, what you are giving up, what you are getting in return, and what you have to believe for the decision to make sense.</strong></p>
