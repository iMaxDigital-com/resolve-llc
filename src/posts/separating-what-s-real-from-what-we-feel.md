  <h1>Separating What's Real from What We Feel</h1>

  <p>Your business partner hasn’t responded to an important email. You’ve been frustrated with him lately, and his silence feels like one more example of how little regard he has for your concerns. By the time you pick up the phone, you’re already preparing for an argument.</p>

  <p>What do you actually know? You sent an email and haven’t received a response. You may also know that this has happened before. But why he hasn’t responded, what he thinks about your concerns, and whether he’s deliberately ignoring you may still be open questions.</p>

  <p>Somewhere between the unanswered email and the phone call, you supplied an explanation. And that explanation began to feel like a fact.</p>

  <p>Fortunately, I became particularly aware of this dynamic during my divorce. There was plenty to feel strongly about, and I could find myself drawing conclusions about motives or intentions that I couldn’t actually know. I developed a habit of checking in with myself: “Am I sure of this? Or am I conflating what is real with shit I’m making up?”</p>

  <p>I also had a very close friend whom I trusted, with whom I could speak unfiltered, and whose opinion and counsel I valued tremendously. I could put my feelings and my version of events on the table, and we could examine them together. My friend’s perspective helped me separate what I knew from what I was assuming—and think more clearly about what to do next.</p>

  <p>I see versions of this in business disputes, family disagreements, and consequential personal decisions. The interpretation comes so quickly that we barely notice we’ve made one. “She questioned my recommendation” becomes “She doesn’t trust me.” “My brother wants a larger share” becomes “He’s always been greedy.” Before long, we’re responding to everything we believe the event says about the other person, our relationship, and what comes next.</p>

  <p>Our history with someone matters. An unanswered email after years of reliable communication lands differently from one that follows months of avoidance. Experience gives us reasons to suspect certain explanations. Still, there is value in recognizing the difference between a reasonable suspicion and something we know.</p>

  <p>Feelings deserve attention, too. Anger may tell us that something feels unfair. Fear may reveal what we believe we could lose. Hurt may show us how much a relationship matters. But the intensity of a feeling doesn’t establish the accuracy of the explanation attached to it. Feeling dismissed doesn’t, by itself, tell us whether someone intended to dismiss us.</p>

  <p>The distinction matters because our explanations influence our next move. If I’m certain you’re trying to take advantage of me, I’m likely to approach you defensively. I may accuse you, threaten you, or stop sharing information. You respond to that behavior, and your response becomes further evidence that I was right about you. We can help create the very situation we were afraid of.</p>

  <p>When I work with someone facing a difficult conflict or decision, part of the work is slowing this process down enough to examine it. What happened? What are we making it mean? What are we feeling? What remains unknown?</p>

  <p>Someone may realize that the intention they’ve attributed to another person has never been tested in conversation. Or they may recognize that their concern is supported by a consistent pattern of behavior. Either discovery provides a firmer basis for deciding what to do next.</p>

  <p>Sometimes the next move is a question: “I haven’t heard back, and we need to make this decision by Friday. Is there something holding this up?” Sometimes it’s a boundary: “We’ve missed this deadline three times. I need us to agree on how decisions will get made.” You don’t need to prove someone’s motives to address the effect of their behavior.</p>

  <p>The same discipline helps when the conflict is internal. “I’m afraid I’ll regret leaving” can quietly become “Leaving would be a mistake.” The fear belongs in the decision. So do the facts, the tradeoffs, and an honest assessment of what we cannot know in advance.</p>

  <p>We rarely get complete certainty before we have to act. But we can become clearer about what supports our judgment and where we’re filling in the gaps. For me, that has meant both asking myself an uncomfortable question and being willing to hear the perspective of someone I trust.</p>

  <p>You don’t have to stop feeling what you feel to examine what you believe. But it is important to separate what is real from what you feel. Often, that is where a better decision begins.</p>
