<p>There is a moment in many difficult situations when doing nothing begins to feel impossible.</p>

<p>Something has happened. A conversation has gone badly. An email arrives that seems to demand an answer. A business partner has taken an action you didn&rsquo;t expect. Someone has said something that feels unfair, threatening, or simply wrong. The instinct is almost immediate: <em>I need to do something.</em></p>

<p>Over the years, I&rsquo;ve become increasingly interested in that moment.</p>

<p>Sometimes action is exactly what&rsquo;s required. A problem ignored can become a larger problem, and avoiding a difficult decision doesn&rsquo;t make the decision disappear. Time continues to pass, circumstances change, and other people make choices of their own. Choosing not to decide can have consequences every bit as real as making an affirmative choice.</p>

<p>But I&rsquo;ve also seen the opposite. I&rsquo;ve watched intelligent, thoughtful people take actions they later regretted, not because they had considered the alternatives and chosen poorly, but because the pressure of the situation made action itself feel necessary. They sent the email, made the accusation, issued the ultimatum, called the lawyer, ended the relationship.</p>

<p>Something had to be done. Or at least it felt that way.</p>

<p>In <a href="https://www.resolve-advisory.com/insights/why-smart-people-make-bad-decisions-under-pressure/"><em>Judgment Under Pressure</em></a>, I wrote about how pressure can change the way we see a situation, narrowing perspective and creating a sense of urgency that can easily be mistaken for clarity. That same pressure can affect not only <em>what</em> we decide, but <em>when</em> we believe we must decide it.</p>

<p>Urgency and necessity are not the same thing.</p>

<p>Neither are failing to make a decision and deciding not to act.</p>

<p>The first can be avoidance. We don&rsquo;t know what to do, don&rsquo;t want to confront the consequences, or simply hope the problem will somehow resolve itself. In those situations, doing nothing isn&rsquo;t really a strategy. It&rsquo;s allowing time and circumstances to make the decision for us.</p>

<p>Deciding not to act can be something entirely different. Sometimes it is restraint: recognizing that we don&rsquo;t yet know enough, that emotions are running too high, that an immediate response is unlikely to improve the situation, or simply that a decision that feels urgent today may look very different tomorrow.</p>

<p>Doing nothing, in those circumstances, isn&rsquo;t the absence of a decision. It is the decision.</p>

<p>The difficult part is knowing the difference.</p>

<p>Avoidance and restraint can look remarkably similar from the outside. In both cases, the email remains unanswered, the conversation doesn&rsquo;t happen, and nothing appears to be moving forward. Internally, however, they are very different. Avoidance postpones a decision because we don&rsquo;t want to confront it. Deliberate restraint postpones action because we <em>have</em> confronted the decision and concluded that acting now is unlikely to improve the situation.</p>

<p>I know this not only from watching other people. I&rsquo;ve done both. I&rsquo;ve acted when I would have been better served by waiting, and I&rsquo;ve waited when I should have acted. Some of those decisions proved costly, financially and personally.</p>

<p>Experience doesn&rsquo;t necessarily make these choices easier, but it can make us more attentive to the questions we should be asking before we make them.</p>

<p>When I find myself feeling that something must be done immediately, I&rsquo;ve learned to become curious about the urgency itself. What happens if I wait? What information might I learn? Is there actually a deadline, or have I created one in my own mind? What is the cost of waiting, and what is the cost of acting too soon?</p>

<p>Sometimes delay carries very real consequences. A contractual right can expire. Money can disappear. A business can deteriorate. Someone else can make a decision that changes the choices available to us. Sometimes the window for action really is closing.</p>

<p>But many difficult situations don&rsquo;t operate on that kind of clock.</p>

<p><strong>The pressure is real, but the deadline isn&rsquo;t.</strong></p>

<p>In those situations, time can become an asset rather than an enemy. An emotion that feels overwhelming today may become more manageable tomorrow. Information that isn&rsquo;t available now may emerge. The other person&rsquo;s position may change. A response that initially seemed obvious may begin to look unnecessarily provocative. An option that wasn&rsquo;t visible at first may appear.</p>

<p>None of this means difficult decisions should be postponed indefinitely. If the decision not to act is deliberate, it should be revisited deliberately. <em>I&rsquo;m not going to respond today</em> is very different from <em>I&rsquo;m not going to deal with this.</em> One is a decision about timing. The other may simply be avoidance.</p>

<p>Years spent making investment decisions taught me a useful way of thinking about this. Investors generally have three choices: buy, hold, or sell. We don&rsquo;t think of holding as the absence of a decision. By continuing to own an investment, we remain exposed to whatever happens next. Holding is a position.</p>

<p>I&rsquo;ve come to think difficult personal and business decisions work much the same way. We can act. We can wait. Or we can decide to do nothing.</p>

<p>All three are positions, and all three have consequences.</p>

<p><strong>There is nothing neutral about inaction.</strong></p>

<p>That doesn&rsquo;t mean we must constantly be doing something. Quite the opposite. Sometimes the best decision available is to leave something alone. But if we&rsquo;re waiting deliberately, we ought to know why. What are we waiting for? What do we expect to learn? What might change? When will we reconsider the decision?</p>

<p>Without answers to those questions, waiting can quietly become drifting.</p>

<p>And while we drift, the situation doesn&rsquo;t necessarily wait with us. Relationships continue to change. Businesses continue to operate. Other people make decisions. Opportunities disappear. Resentments harden. Even silence can acquire meaning for someone on the other side.</p>

<p>Which leaves another possibility: failing to decide.</p>

<p>Acting, waiting, and doing nothing can all be deliberate choices. Failing to decide is different. It allows circumstances, time, or other people to make choices that we have declined to make ourselves.</p>

<p>I&rsquo;ve learned that good judgment isn&rsquo;t always knowing when to act. Sometimes it requires the discipline not to act. At other times, it requires recognizing that what we&rsquo;ve been calling patience has become avoidance.</p>

<p>The question isn&rsquo;t whether doing something is better than doing nothing. It&rsquo;s whether either one is the product of judgment rather than reaction.</p>

<p>So when I hear myself thinking, <em>Something must be done</em>, I&rsquo;ve learned to pay attention to the word <em>must</em>. Maybe something does need to be done, and maybe it needs to be done now. But sometimes the better first decision is to stop long enough to ask what choice I&rsquo;m actually making.</p>

<p>Act.</p>

<p>Wait.</p>

<p>Do nothing.</p>

<p>Each can be the right decision. Each can be the wrong one.</p>

<p><strong>And each belongs to me.</strong></p>
