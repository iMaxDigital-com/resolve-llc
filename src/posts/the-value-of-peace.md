<h1>The Value of Peace</h1>

<p>Years ago, when I first began thinking about the practice that eventually became Resolve Advisory, I called it Shalem Strategies. <em>Shalem</em> is a Hebrew word (like <em>shalom</em>) associated with wholeness, completeness, and peace. I eventually changed the name, but I never really left the idea behind.</p>

<p>One of the questions I find myself returning to in difficult conflicts is deceptively simple: <strong>What would peace be worth?</strong></p>

<p>We don't usually think about conflict this way. When we're in the middle of a dispute, we tend to focus on the things we can see and measure. How much money is at stake? What am I entitled to? What would constitute a fair settlement? What would it cost to hire a lawyer? What happens if I give in?</p>

<p>Those are important questions. But they leave something important off the ledger: the cost of continuing the conflict.</p>

<h2>Conflict Has a Carrying Cost</h2>

<p>An unresolved conflict occupies space. It takes time and consumes attention. It follows you into other parts of your life. You replay conversations, compose responses in your head, anticipate what the other person might do next, and talk about it with your spouse, friends, business partners, or advisors. Sometimes you wake up in the middle of the night thinking about it.</p>

<p>There are financial costs, of course: lawyers, accountants, consultants, missed opportunities, delayed transactions, businesses that cannot make decisions because their owners cannot agree. But some of the largest costs never appear on an invoice.</p>

<p>There is the management attention diverted from running a business, the family gathering everyone dreads, the relationship that deteriorates a little more with each exchange, and the decision that cannot be made while the dispute remains unresolved. There is also simply the cost of having the conflict live in your head.</p>

<p>In investing, we understand the concept of carrying cost. Holding something has a cost even when nothing appears to be happening. Conflict can work the same way. Doing nothing doesn't necessarily mean the cost has stopped accumulating.</p>

<p>As I explored in <a href="https://www.resolve-advisory.com/insights/When-Doing-Nothing-Is-Actually-a-Decision">When Doing Nothing Is Actually a Decision</a>, choosing not to act is still a choice. In an unresolved conflict, that choice can have a cost of its own.</p>

<h2>We Know How to Value Other Intangibles</h2>

<p>In business and investing, we routinely assign value to things that are difficult to see. We understand that liquidity, certainty, optionality, reducing risk, and time itself can all have economic value.</p>

<p>Yet in conflict, the calculation often becomes much narrower: <strong>What am I giving up?</strong></p>

<p>If I accept $400,000 instead of the $500,000 I believe I deserve, I have given up $100,000. That's easy to calculate. Much harder to calculate is what I receive in exchange.</p>

<p>Perhaps the dispute ends today rather than eighteen months from now. The legal bills stop. Your attention returns to your business. You no longer have to interact regularly with someone you have come to dread. You eliminate the risk of a much worse outcome and can begin making plans again.</p>

<p>That doesn't automatically make the $400,000 offer a good one. But those things have value too, and they belong in the calculation.</p>

<h2>Peace Doesn't Always Mean Reconciliation</h2>

<p>The word <em>peace</em> can sound sentimental in the context of serious conflict. That isn't what I mean. Peace doesn't require two people to hug, forgive each other, or agree about what happened.</p>

<p>It might mean reconciliation, but it could just as easily mean reaching a settlement, selling your interest in a business, establishing a boundary, or accepting that you are never going to receive the apology you believe you deserve. Sometimes it means walking away.</p>

<p>The common element is not harmony. It is reaching a point where the conflict no longer controls what happens next.</p>

<h2>Peace at Any Price Isn't Peace</h2>

<p>If peace has value, does that mean you should always compromise? Of course not. There are circumstances in which the right decision is to stand your ground.</p>

<p>A principle may genuinely matter. The economic difference may be too large. The precedent you establish may have consequences. Giving in may invite additional demands rather than end them. Sometimes the cost of peace really is too high.</p>

<p>But that is different from failing to calculate its value at all. The objective isn't to avoid conflict. It is to make a deliberate decision about whether continuing the conflict is worth what it is costing you.</p>

<h2>Put Peace on the Ledger</h2>

<p>When people are caught in difficult conflicts, I sometimes find it useful to change the question. Instead of asking only, <strong>What will I have to give up to end this?</strong> ask, <strong>What will I get back if this ends?</strong></p>

<p>The answer might include your time and attention, the ability to focus on your business, a relationship you still care about, the freedom to pursue something else, or simply the ability to sleep without rehearsing tomorrow's argument. The answer will be different for everyone, and not everything needs to be converted into dollars. But it deserves to be counted.</p>

<p>Sometimes the difference between a good decision and a bad one isn't discovering another argument, another negotiating tactic, or another way to win. It's recognizing the value of no longer having to fight.</p>

<p>Peace has value. <strong>The question is whether you've accounted for it.</strong></p>
